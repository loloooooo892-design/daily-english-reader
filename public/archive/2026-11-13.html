John Cusack Goes Graphic

For the actor John Cusack to deliver an apathetic Cameo performance—Cameo being the service through which regular people hire famous people to make short videos wishing a friend happy birthday, or whatever—feels like the most John Cusack-character thing that John Cusack could do. But “the internet” (meaning Threads) recently turned against him for phoning it in on Cameo. Cusack, however, at sixty years old, still gets plenty amped up about the things that actually interest him.

Things that currently interest John Cusack include politics, fringe philosopher-artists, and graphic novels, one of which he recently published. “Momo,” illustrated by Ignacio Noé, is Cusack’s noirish seventies road-trip romp about the philosopher-artist Antonin Artaud (per Cusack: “sovereign of the absurd, master of the irrational, lord of the permanent rebellion”), who spent years in French mental institutions. Cusack’s book imagines a scenario in which Artaud fakes his own death and is sprung from an asylum by Jackie Gleason, who then accompanies him and Richard Nixon to the Homestead Air Force Base to check out embalmed alien heads. After that, things get really weird.

The other day, at Forbidden Planet, a comic-book store near Union Square, Cusack, dressed in black (uniform, not disguise), moved easily among young punks in plaid and Doc Martens and clerks in ironic tees. He said that the artist Damien Hirst (whom he met in the nineties, with Joe Strummer) had turned him on to Noé’s work. “You can always go to Damien and get this Wonka world,” Cusack said, in his flat, gravelly voice, punctuated by a slow lemur blink. He recalled, “Last time I was at his place, he had bought a graveyard in Scotland. And he was building a house out of the headstones. I said, ‘It’s going to be haunted.’ And he said, ‘It will be when I’m fucking done with it.’ ”

Cusack perused the shelves. “I read ‘Dr. Strange,’ ” he said. “I love the old ‘Creepshow,’ ‘Daredevil.’ ” “Momo” takes place in 1972, when Cusack was six. “But I remember stuff,” he said. “Elvis was on TV, Watergate.” The book came pretty easy: “It was like writing a script, except I had to negotiate a lot less with anybody else. It was like a movie that only existed in my mind.”

He stood in front of boxed, beefy action figurines and opined on Artaud, who, notably, had freaked out even his Surrealist contemporaries. “He just went all the way,” Cusack said. “And what he wanted was always almost impossible—he wanted performance to be a magic spell. I always thought he was this great avatar that represented success and failure—failure mostly. Like Sid Vicious but much more talented. I always thought that, in a way, he was a wizard.”

What would an Artaud action figure look like? (He’s known to have had a “magic” walking stick.) Cusack studied a Starman figurine and mused, “I think if we did monetize Artaud as an action figure, he would come out of the primordial abyss and stomp on me.” He walked past a doormat that read “GODZILLA WAS HERE” and featured a big reptilian footprint.

Heading up Broadway—a former stomping ground, from his N.Y.U. days—Cusack pointed. “That used to be a theatre,” he said. “There was a play where everybody was on wires. They used to come down and grab you.”

He settled in at a table at Union Square Café, removing his cap and ruffling his hair. “Mômo,” he explained, was a moniker that Artaud gave himself: “It was some alter ego or something.” Cusack said that none of his friends call him by his name. “People called me Carl for a long time,” he said. “Or Doctor, like, a self-proclaimed doctor of whatever.” He ordered an “iced-tea lemonade.” “Like an Arnold Palmer?” the waitress asked.
