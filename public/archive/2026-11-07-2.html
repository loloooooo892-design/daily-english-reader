“Paper Tiger” Views Family as Deadweight

There are no ticking clocks or flipping calendar pages in James Gray’s new film, “Paper Tiger,” but it’s nonetheless a drama of time implacably passing. Call it a September song: the story opens on Labor Day in 1986, in Queens, at the home of the Pearl family. Early in the morning, the jovial, still youthful paterfamilias, Irwin (Miles Teller), has his power tools going in the narrow driveway beside the house, waking his sons—Scott (Gavin Goudey), who’s about to turn eighteen, and Ben (Roman Engel), maybe five years younger—on their last day of summer vacation. As Jerry Lewis’s annual telethon blares from a TV in the kitchen, Irwin and his wife, Hester (Scarlett Johansson), tell the boys that they’re all going to visit Hester’s mother, Iris (Cindy Katz), at a country club near her home, in the prosperous and largely Jewish Long Island suburb of Great Neck.

From this banal beginning, Gray launches a tale of fear and violence, criminal conspiracies and paranoia. That night, after the family gets back home, Irwin’s older brother, Gary (Adam Driver), arrives with princely pomp, bringing a takeout feast from the venerable steak house Peter Luger. A former high-ranking cop now earning a grand living as a security consultant—and basking in the admiration of his former N.Y.P.D. colleagues—Gary, who drives a Mercedes and is seemingly sewn into sharply tailored suits, is as dashing as Irwin is staid. The fancy meal is the prelude to a proposition: he wants Irwin, an environmental engineer, to team up with him as a co-consultant to the owners of a grossly polluted site on the Gowanus Canal, in Brooklyn, which is about to undergo a federally funded cleanup. Gary is sure that the job will be lucrative and tells Irwin that the owners, recently arrived Russians whom he describes as scientists and engineers, are in need of Irwin’s regulatory and technical expertise.

At a preliminary meeting in Gowanus, little about the prospective clients’ rough manner and crude appearance bears out Gary’s claims. And a few days later, when the overeager Irwin, following his usual procedure, visits the site to take stock—proudly bringing his sons along to see the world of his work—he finds that he’s dealing with gangsters of rare ruthlessness and brutality. By this time, though, he and Gary are in too deep: the gangsters beat Irwin and menace Hester and the boys. To protect the family, Gary goes into heavy debt to the syndicate; the movie’s title comes from the description he offers of his gangland creditors to allay Irwin’s fears. “I’m N.Y.P.D.,” Gary says, adding, “They’re not gonna touch me.” “But they can touch me,” Irwin skeptically retorts. As the days until payment is due tick grimly by, Irwin and Hester grow increasingly fretful and angry, and their widening divisions wrench the family apart, to intimately catastrophic effect.

Such lovequakes are a through line of Gray’s directorial career, which began in 1994, with “Little Odessa.” Gray, one of cinema’s great homebodies, grew up in Queens, and most of his movies are set there or in Brooklyn and deal bruisingly with the financial tensions, thwarted dreams, and frayed bonds of middle-class Jewish families like the one in which he was raised—stressed, streetwise, and crime-proximate. His previous film, “Armageddon Time,” from 2022—to my mind, his hitherto most accomplished one—is, like “Paper Tiger,” set in his home neighborhood, Fresh Meadows. That film takes place in 1980, with frequent allusions to Ronald Reagan’s ongoing Presidential campaign, and it focusses on a family very similar to the one in “Paper Tiger”: a father named Irving, who is a contractor; a mother named Esther; and two sons, the younger of whom transfers from public school to a nearby private day school dominated by a wealthy local family—the Trumps.

Gray conceived “Paper Tiger” as a sequel to “Armageddon Time” but altered his plans when the actors who played the parents in the earlier film, Anne Hathaway and Jeremy Strong, were unavailable. The two works remain cognate, but their differences are crucial. Strong’s Irving was tougher, more abrasive than Irwin is; conversely, Hathaway’s Esther, a teacher, was relatively refined and educated, whereas Johansson’s counterpart sleeps with rollers in her hair, like a vestige of the pre-Beatles sixties; speaks in a Queens accent that could cut metal; and holds the family together with her life-worn smarts and iron will. Yet she’s as unworldly as the nerdy Irwin, looking up to him with his fund of “facts and figures.”

But fires of rage burn below the surface of “Paper Tiger,” exemplified in the opening visit to Great Neck. Iris wants Hester and her family to move there; Hester demurs, explaining that college tuition for the boys must come first, but Irwin openly scoffs: “Everyone here was born with a silver spoon in their mouth, or stole one.” Iris, seeing suburban luxury as her daughter’s birthright, thinks that Hester’s marriage has been a comedown. The Pearls aren’t poor, but they’re burdened by expenses and tantalized by comforts just out of reach, especially now that their sons’ heads have been turned by Gary’s flashy success. (Comparing his uncle’s Mercedes with his parents’ cars, Scott whines, “Why do we always have to buy crappy stuff and live crappy?”)

Irwin’s run-in with the Mob dispels the fantasy of easy money; instead, he packs suitcases and makes plans to get the family out of town for their safety. Yet “Paper Tiger” is far more than a tale of criminal violence piercing the domestic bubble. It’s a story of silences, ones that form a conspicuous contrast with its precursor. “Armageddon Time,” with its expressly political context, is a film of rich discourse, filled with rhetorical expositions of ideology and history, reminiscences and commentaries. In “Paper Tiger,” there’s plenty of talk throughout, but hardly any of it involves cultural context, broader observation, or deeper reflection; it’s not a film of ideas. Instead, the heated dialogue is an expression of the inner life of the family and its time.

Interviews with directors have become a familiar part of movie promotion, but Gray’s interviews are different, often less like conversations than like performances. He’s among the most publicly funny of filmmakers, a gifted impersonator; I’ve long hoped that he would direct a comedy (he hasn’t yet) and appear in it. But his talent for mimicry comes across thrillingly in the dialogue of “Paper Tiger”—Gray wrote the screenplay himself—which adds an extra dimension to the story’s autobiographical elements. Both in ordinary chitchat and in big confrontations, the dialogue plays like a musical score of language built from a trove of memories. The film’s wondrously street-lyrical patter, its emblematic phrases and inflections, amount to a vast act of world-building—a lost milieu brought back to life in bygone clichés.

Much of this evocative talk also proves evasive. Just as Gary’s forecast for the Gowanus venture turns out to have been misleading, the fallout from that calamitous misadventure quickly brings on further elisions and secrets, transforming a happy home and a stable marriage into a curtained stage of furtive designs, hidden agendas, and stealthy exploits. Gary’s Mephistophelian temptations break the façades of business, law, and marriage, revealing the profound and irreparable solitude that lurks beneath life’s fundamental bonds.

Gray has always displayed a probing visual style, boldly confrontational yet painfully tender. In “Paper Tiger,” he refines these tendencies into lapidary images—spare, taut, and precise—that evoke the fierce solidity of the family circle and the fearsome emptiness that’s left when it shatters. With a quietly dazzling repertory of shadows and reflections, fragmented glimpses, truncated vistas, and blocked perspectives, he conjures everything the film keeps hidden, the illusions and limitations that inhere in frank gazes and point-blank views. I’ve seen “Paper Tiger” on the big screen but am looking forward to its streaming release in order to rewatch it as a silent film. Amid all its chatter, Gray has found the space to let images be eloquent, too. And their eloquence is greatest when expressing the silence and solitude of Hester.

In this regard, Gray’s movie resembles a pair of recent classics by Martin Scorsese, “The Irishman” and “Killers of the Flower Moon,” both of which feature women who respond to betrayal by men with silences deafening enough to rend the films’ finely textured surfaces. In “Paper Tiger,” the cycle of tragedy finds its definitive voice in Hester’s reproachful silences, which turn out to be radical rejections of the tight little world she inhabits. Determined and relentless, Hester, as incarnated by Johansson, dominates the action and steals the film with a decisive form of passive aggression—one that Gray condenses into a breathtakingly rapid, boldly symbolic special effect. By the time September winds down, Hester is on a timetable all her own, out of synch with her family, detached from its cares, paradoxically and bitterly free. ♦
