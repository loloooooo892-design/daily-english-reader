Even "Trumpy Judges" and "Woke Judges" Might Know More Law Than You Do

| 10.29.2026 3:42 PM

I appreciate that many people are skeptical about judges. Indeed, it's good that people take with a grain of salt the statements of government officials, including judges. Judges are human, and humans make mistakes and have prejudices and blind spots.

At the same time, judges are generally quite well-trained lawyers, and know a good deal about what they're writing about. Conventional wisdom holds that federal judges generally are particularly well-qualified (of course, with exceptions, as is true for all institutions). Federal judges also generally employ quite well-qualified law clerks, who do much of the  research and initial opinion drafting.

This means that, for all the risk of error and ideological bias, judges generally (though not always) know what they're talking about in their legal analysis. They tend to particularly know more than non-lawyers, but also often more than lawyers who aren't specialists in the particular field. You might assume that some judge is "woke" or "Trumpy" or what have you, but there's a pretty good chance that the judge's legal analysis is still correct.

This post was prompted by a comment I saw on the Eric Coomer (of Dominion Voting) Loses Libel Lawsuit over The Deep Rig post, though this is just one example of a broader trend. The comment quotes an excerpt from the post's excerpt of the judge's opinion:

In short, if the law did not require "clear and convincing evidence" that the defendant subjectively doubted or disbelieved the published statements, it is likely that Byrne's motion for summary judgment would have been denied, and a jury would have had the opportunity to weigh in on this matter. However, under the law as it currently stands, Coomer's evidence is insufficient to meet these standards and summary judgment for Byrne is therefore required….

The comment then says, "Trumpy judge who apparently didn't bother to read Sullivan, which states" (emphasis added by commenter),

The constitutional guarantees require, we think, a federal rule that prohibits a public official from recovering damages for a defamatory falsehood relating to his official conduct unless he proves that the statement was made with "actual malice" — that is, with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not.

And the comment continues, "Seems like this is ripe for appeal. This Trumpy judge seems to think the current precedent requires that the defendant 'subjectively doubted or disbelieved the published statements,' when Sullivan has a looser standard."

The trouble with the commenter's analysis is that Sullivan was of course not the last word on the subject—as the judge knew. Indeed, if one searches for "subjective" in the judge's opinion, one sees a longish passage citing authority supporting the judge's analysis:

Actual malice requires proof that the defendant published the challenged statement with knowledge of its falsity or with reckless disregard for whether it was false. See id. [Coomer v. Donald J. Trump for President, Inc., 552 P.3d 562, 581 (Colo. App. 2024)]; New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 279-80 (1964); St. Amant v. Thompson, 390 U.S. 727, 731 (1968). The test for actual malice is a subjective one. Thus, the issue is not whether "a reasonably prudent [person] would have published, or would have investigated before publishing," but rather whether the defendant "in fact entertained serious doubts as to the truth of [the] publication, or was highly aware of its probable falsity[.]" Coomer, 552 P.3d at 591; Diversified Mgmt., Inc. v. The Denver Post, Inc., 653 P.2d 1103. 1105-10 (Colo. 1982) (en banc); Michel v. NYP Holdings, Inc., 816 F.3d 686, 703 (11th Cir. 2016) ("[W]e ask whether the defendant, instead of acting in good faith, actually entertained serious doubts as to the veracity of the published account, or was highly aware that the account was probably false."); Dershowitz v. Cable News Network, Inc., 153 F.4th 1189, 1193 (11th Cir. 2025) ("[A]ll that matters are the speakers' subjective beliefs about the truth of their own statements.").

St. Amant, cited by the court, indeed states,

[The precedents] are clear that reckless conduct is not measured by whether a reasonably prudent man would have published, or would have investigated before publishing. There must be sufficient evidence to permit the conclusion that the defendant in fact entertained serious doubts as to the truth of his publication. Publishing with such doubts shows reckless disregard for truth or falsity and demonstrates actual malice.

Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc. (1974) expressly quotes this passage as the Court having "equated reckless disregard of the truth with subjective awareness of probable falsity." The judge in the Coomer / Deep Rig case, whether you view him as "Trumpy" or not, knew the law here, and the commenter (who had elsewhere said he wasn't a lawyer) didn't seem to.

So, again, definitely view judges' work skeptically, and feel free to criticize it. But don't assume that just because you know one precedent in the field you know the law better than the judge. Don't assume that just because you think the judge has some ideological bias, that ideological bias has led the judge into legal error. And if you have some skepticism about the judge's analysis, check the sources that the judge cites in the full opinion; my posts usually just excerpt the opinion, and often don't include citations, but they generally link to the full opinion so readers can consult it directly.
